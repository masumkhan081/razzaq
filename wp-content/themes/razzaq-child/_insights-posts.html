Section 24 and the property investor: what the mortgage interest restriction actually means Insights · Property Tax 2026-04-15 https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1554224155-6726b3ff858f?w=1800&q=85 Landlords can no longer deduct mortgage interest as a business expense. We explain the impact for higher-rate taxpayers and the structures that can mitigate it — incorporation, spousal transfers, SPVs and reduced leverage.

Section 24 of the Finance (No. 2) Act 2015 introduced one of the most significant changes to UK property taxation in a generation. For individual landlords of residential property, mortgage interest is no longer a deductible expense against rental income. Instead, a basic-rate tax reducer — equal to 20% of the interest — is given against the final tax bill.

The practical effect is that rental profit is now calculated on gross rents less non-finance expenses. A landlord receiving £30,000 of rent and paying £20,000 of mortgage interest — with £3,000 of other expenses — previously had taxable profit of £7,000. Today, that landlord has taxable profit of £27,000 (with a £4,000 tax reducer). For a higher-rate taxpayer, the difference is significant.

Who is affected, and how much

The restriction applies to individuals (including those in partnership) letting residential property. It does not apply to:

  • Limited companies holding investment property
  • Commercial property let by individuals
  • Qualifying furnished holiday lets (though the FHL regime itself is under review)

For higher-rate and additional-rate taxpayers, the economic effect is that finance costs are effectively relieved at 20% rather than 40% or 45%. Where a portfolio is heavily leveraged, it is entirely possible for the tax bill to exceed the net cash generated — a scenario colloquially known as a "negative yield" position.

The restriction changes not only the tax bill, but the calculation of total income — which can push landlords into additional-rate territory, trigger the High Income Child Benefit Charge, or reduce the personal savings allowance.

What are the mitigation options?

Transfer to a lower-rate spouse

Where one spouse is a basic-rate taxpayer, transferring the property (or a share of it) to that spouse can materially reduce the overall tax burden. The transfer itself is generally CGT-neutral between spouses, but SDLT consequences and mortgage-lender consent need careful handling.

Incorporation

Limited companies are not subject to Section 24 and can still deduct mortgage interest in full. However, incorporating an existing portfolio is not trivial — it can trigger substantial CGT (potentially mitigated by Section 162 incorporation relief where the activity qualifies as a business) and SDLT (potentially mitigated where the partnership-to-company route is available). Incorporation is sometimes the right answer and sometimes a very expensive mistake.

Buy new property through a limited company

For new acquisitions, using a special-purpose vehicle (SPV) company is increasingly the default for higher-rate investors. The tradeoffs include: higher mortgage rates (typically), corporation tax on profits, and tax on extraction of funds as dividends. The long-term arithmetic generally favours the SPV for investors retaining property for growth.

Reduce leverage

For some landlords, simply paying down the mortgage is the most tax-efficient response. The after-tax return on debt reduction can be compelling where Section 24 has pushed effective tax rates higher.

The bottom line

There is no one-size-fits-all answer to Section 24. The right structure depends on your existing portfolio, your rate of tax, your cash requirements, your time horizon and the specific mortgage products available to you. What is certain is that doing nothing — and continuing to absorb the restriction at higher rates — is rarely optimal for portfolios of any meaningful size.

High Income Child Benefit Charge: who it affects and how to manage it Insights · Self Assessment 2026-03-12 https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1450101499163-c8848c66ca85?w=1800&q=85 The HICBC threshold rose to £60,000 in April 2024, with the taper extending to £80,000. Many families are still unaware. We set out the rules and the practical options to reduce or eliminate the charge.

The High Income Child Benefit Charge (HICBC) was introduced in 2013. In short: where either partner in a household earns above a threshold, the family's entitlement to Child Benefit is clawed back through a tax charge on that higher earner. From April 2024, the threshold was raised from £50,000 to £60,000 and the taper widened to £80,000 (previously £60,000).

How the charge works

The charge is based on adjusted net income (broadly, total income less pension contributions and gift aid). It operates as follows:

  • Below £60,000: no charge — full Child Benefit retained
  • £60,000 – £80,000: tapered charge at 1% of Child Benefit for every £200 over £60,000
  • Above £80,000: charge equals 100% of Child Benefit — effectively neutralising the payment

Crucially, the charge falls on the higher earner — even if Child Benefit is claimed by their partner. Where both parties are unaware of the rules, this is a common source of unexpected tax bills and penalties.

Common issues we see

The "unexpected payslip bump"

Employees whose income has drifted above £60,000 — via bonuses, overtime, benefits in kind or a pay rise — are often unaware they have crossed the threshold until HMRC issues a discovery assessment. Where Self Assessment has not been filed because the taxpayer was otherwise PAYE-only, this can trigger failure-to-notify penalties on top of the charge itself.

The "opt out" assumption

Many parents opt out of receiving Child Benefit to avoid the charge. This is sometimes sensible — but carries a hidden cost: opting out can affect National Insurance credits for the non-earning partner (relevant for state pension entitlement). The correct approach is usually to claim and repay, not opt out.

Opting out of Child Benefit to avoid a tax charge can cost more in state pension than it saves in tax. Claim, then repay through Self Assessment.

How to manage HICBC

Pension contributions

Personal pension contributions reduce adjusted net income on a gross basis. Contributing £8,000 net (£10,000 gross) directly into a personal pension can bring income from £70,000 down to £60,000 — eliminating the charge entirely while also attracting higher-rate relief. This is often the most efficient single intervention.

Salary sacrifice

Where available, salary sacrifice into pension reduces gross pay and therefore adjusted net income. This also saves employer NIC — some employers share that saving.

Gift aid donations

Gift aid donations similarly reduce adjusted net income. For charitable givers, claiming properly through Self Assessment delivers higher-rate relief and reduces HICBC exposure in the same move.

Timing of income

For directors with control over dividend timing, deferring a dividend from March to April can shift the income into a different tax year — potentially avoiding a threshold crossing in a particularly high year.

If you have missed registering

HMRC is actively reviewing PAYE cases where HICBC has been underpaid. If you think you have missed reporting — whether by one year or several — the best approach is to make a voluntary disclosure promptly. Penalties are significantly reduced where the taxpayer comes forward rather than waiting for HMRC to make contact.

MTD for VAT: are you fully compliant with Making Tax Digital requirements? Insights · VAT 2026-02-18 https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1460925895917-afdab827c52f?w=1800&q=85 Making Tax Digital for VAT has been in force since 2019 — but we still see workflows that fall short of the digital-link requirement. The rules are stricter than many realise.

Since April 2019, VAT-registered businesses above the threshold have been required to keep digital records and file VAT returns through MTD-compatible software. Since April 2022, the requirement has extended to all VAT-registered businesses, including those voluntarily registered below the threshold. Despite this, a surprising number of businesses still operate workflows that fall short of full compliance.

What "compliant" actually requires

MTD for VAT has three core requirements:

  1. Digital records — a digital record of each sale and purchase, held in "functional compatible software"
  2. Digital links — where data moves between software products (e.g. from a spreadsheet to a bridging tool), the transfer must be via an API, import, or linked cells — never manual retyping or cut-and-paste
  3. Digital submission — the return itself must be submitted via API to HMRC, not keyed into the old portal

The first and third are widely understood. The digital link requirement is where we see most non-compliance in practice.

Common gaps we encounter

Spreadsheet totals typed into bridging software

A business keeps records in Excel. At quarter-end, the bookkeeper types the nine box figures into a bridging tool. This breaks the digital link and is not compliant — even though every file along the way is "digital".

Sales from a till system manually re-entered into accounts

Where POS data is summarised on paper and keyed into accounting software, the link is broken. POS integrations or structured exports are required.

Adjustments entered directly into the return

Manual adjustments — partial exemption, fuel scale charges, bad debt relief — are often applied at return level. Under MTD these adjustments must themselves be recorded digitally in the accounting records, with the adjustment amount flowing through by digital link.

The principle is simple: once a figure enters the digital system, it must not leave it until it arrives at HMRC. Every step in between must be a digital link.

What are the consequences?

HMRC now has the ability to issue penalties specifically for non-compliance with MTD record-keeping rules — separate from the late-submission penalty regime introduced in 2023. A VAT compliance check can uncover digital-link failures that lead to assessments, penalties, and ongoing visibility for the business.

What should you do?

  • Map your data flow — from the first record of a transaction to the return box
  • Identify manual steps — anywhere a human retypes a figure is a potential break
  • Replace with digital links — API integrations, CSV imports or formula-linked cells in spreadsheets
  • Document your adjustments — ensure partial exemption and other adjustments are recorded within the accounting system

For many small businesses using modern cloud accounting, MTD compliance is straightforward. For businesses using a mix of industry-specific software and Excel, a careful review is worthwhile. Extracting profit from your limited company: salary, dividends, or a combination? Insights · Limited Companies 2026-01-22 https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1556742049-0cfed4f6a45d?w=1800&q=85 The dividend allowance has fallen to £500. The corporation tax main rate is 25%. NIC thresholds have shifted. The optimal remuneration mix for 2026 looks different to what it did even two years ago — a practical guide.

For owner-managers of UK limited companies, the question of how to extract profit — salary, dividends, pension contributions, or some combination — is one of the most consequential annual decisions. Recent changes to corporation tax rates, dividend allowances and NIC thresholds have shifted the calculation meaningfully. What was optimal in 2022 is not optimal in 2026.

The current landscape

  • Corporation tax: 25% main rate above £250,000 profits, 19% small profits rate below £50,000, marginal relief in between (effective 26.5% on the middle band)
  • Dividend tax rates: 8.75% basic rate, 33.75% higher rate, 39.35% additional rate
  • Dividend allowance: £500 (reduced from £2,000 in 2023/24)
  • Personal allowance: £12,570 (frozen)
  • NIC secondary threshold: currently relevant for optimal director salary

The starting point: a small director's salary

For most owner-managers, a salary up to the NIC secondary threshold remains the sensible starting point. This level:

  • Uses the personal allowance efficiently
  • Attracts no employer or employee NIC
  • Is deductible for corporation tax purposes (saving 19–26.5%)
  • Counts as a qualifying year for state pension

For most single-director companies, this is the baseline. The more interesting question is what to take on top — and the answer depends heavily on the rest of your income.

The dividend layer

Once salary is set, further extraction is typically by dividend. The first £500 is covered by the dividend allowance. Dividends above that point are taxed at 8.75%, 33.75% or 39.35% depending on the individual's marginal band.

Because dividends have already suffered corporation tax in the company, the combined effective rate for a higher-rate taxpayer is approximately 45–49%, depending on whether small profits rate or main rate applies — broadly comparable to, and often slightly below, salary + employer/employee NIC at the same band.

The dividend advantage over salary has narrowed significantly in recent years. For many owner-managers, the spread is now measured in percentage points rather than meaningful percentages.

Where pension contributions fit in

Employer pension contributions are frequently the single most efficient form of extraction — particularly for higher earners:

  • Deductible for corporation tax (subject to the wholly & exclusively test)
  • No NIC on employer contributions
  • No income tax on the individual until drawdown (subject to the annual allowance)
  • Significant long-term compounding inside a tax-protected wrapper

For directors with adjusted income above £260,000, the tapered annual allowance restricts this — but up to that point, employer pension contributions of £60,000 per year are a highly efficient extraction route.

Spousal involvement

Where a spouse is genuinely involved in the business (not a sham), splitting shareholdings can allow dividend income to use two personal allowances and two basic-rate bands. The "settlements" anti-avoidance rules require genuine involvement and commercial rationale — but where those are present, the saving can be substantial.

A worked example for 2026

Consider an owner-manager with £120,000 of company profit available and no other income. An efficient structure might be:

  • Director salary at NIC secondary threshold — uses personal allowance efficiently
  • Employer pension contribution of £30,000 — zero tax leakage, long-term wealth accumulation
  • Remaining profit retained in company or distributed as dividend as required

The precise numbers depend on personal circumstances, existing pension pots, cash requirements and attitude to retaining funds inside the company. The optimal mix is not a formula — it requires modelling.
